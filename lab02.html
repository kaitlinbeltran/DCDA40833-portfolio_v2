AI Tool Evaluation

Lab Requirements

This lab focused on developing a structured framework for evaluating artificial intelligence tools. We were asked to analyze at least two AI tools from different categories using four evaluation dimensions: capabilities, appropriate use, ethical considerations, and transparency. The assignment required hands-on experimentation with the tools, documenting prompts and outputs, and writing a 500–750 word analysis discussing both the strengths and limitations of each tool. The goal of the lab was to develop critical thinking skills for evaluating emerging AI technologies rather than simply using them at face value.

Introduction

For Lab 2, I chose to evaluate two AI tools: ChatGPT (a text and chat assistant) and Canva Magic Studio (an AI-powered presentation and design tool) to better understand how artificial intelligence affects modern digital and professional workflows. As an incoming Sales Development Representative at RealPage, I explored these tools within a SaaS sales context, using them to simulate tasks I will likely come across in my job, such as writing sales emails and building proposal decks. By testing a series of prompts, I examined each tool’s capabilities, appropriate use, ethical considerations, and transparency. Through this process, I also reflected on how AI tools connect with the Digital Culture & Data Analytics program, as I enjoy connecting technology to my relevant fields of study.

Tool 1: ChatGPT

Capabilities

When studying ChatGPT, I first assessed its capabilities by asking it to take on the role of a Sales Development Representative at RealPage and to generate a cold email to a CFO at a property management company. The tool was able to quickly produce a professional and realistic email, using relevant B2B SaaS language. This demonstrated ChatGPT’s strength in generating industry-specific written content that can support day-to-day sales operations. I tested this further by inputting a poorly written email and asking ChatGPT to refine it using a specific sales framework, and it impressed me again by responding with a neater, tighter draft that directly followed my instructions.

Appropriate Use

While ChatGPT is successful with email revision, it also brought to mind some counterproductive uses for the tool. After interning in a sales position this summer, one thing I learned was to beware of over-utilizing ChatGPT with outreach, as it can often be obvious that it is generalized. Sales professionals tend to follow the rule of thumb: “If it can be sent to multiple prospects as long as you change the name, it is not personal enough.” For example, ChatGPT’s response below is far too general to send to someone on LinkedIn, as it looks like it could be copied and pasted and sent to hundreds of property management company owners! Copy-and-paste mistakes are another reason not to rely on ChatGPT for outreach generation, as it is (sadly) a common mistake in sales to send a message to a prospect directed to the wrong name or, even worse, a note that starts with “Hi [Insert Name].” Ultimately, ChatGPT is a powerful tool for revision and drafts, but it should not be solely relied upon when it comes to customer outreach techniques (or, outside of the case of sales, it should not be used for assignments or emails in general, as it can easily come across as inauthentic).

Ethical Considerations

The last “test” I ran on ChatGPT revolved around ethics. I prompted the tool to generate an unethical, manipulative email to a prospect. Chat’s response demonstrated that the tool does have built-in safeguards to prevent unethical behaviors. This is definitely a positive when evaluating the tool, as people cannot use it for deception, manipulation, or bullying. The tool has boundaries on how far it can go.

A concern that I came across, however, is its accuracy and relevance. I asked ChatGPT how much RealPage’s stock was, knowing very well that RealPage is a private company. The tool “partially failed” the test, as it does not appear to have any current information. However, the reason I only say it “partially” failed is that it did give a disclaimer at the bottom that live market data would be the most accurate source. This raises the ethical concern that some of ChatGPT’s data is outdated and not currently relevant, which is an issue with accuracy for those who rely on the tool for professional, academic, or research matters.

Tool 2: Canva Magic Studio

Capabilities

Canva Magic Studio was exciting to explore because, as a frequent Canva user, I was never aware that it had an AI tool as well. I began with testing its capabilities, and I was quite impressed with its speed in being able to generate entire designs and presentations.

The two images above (part of the 5-slide presentation the tool generated) were created by Canva Magic Studio in less than a minute. When it comes to time efficiency, Canva Magic Studio is certainly capable.

Appropriate Use

One thing the generated result lacks, however, is specifics. This was expected, as I did not give much detail in the prompt. The most productive use of the feature would be to input a more detailed prompt (with numbers, current research, etc.) and then have the tool create the visual portion for you. (As someone not very talented when it comes to aesthetics and design, I will certainly be utilizing this tool to make my presentations more visually appealing) Overall, Canva Magic Design is a strong tool for presentation visuals and structure, but should not be used to generate information.

Ethical Considerations

While Canva Magic Studio is extremely capable in certain aspects, it does have shortcomings when evaluating ethics. I tested this by asking the tool to create a pitch deck catered to investors that includes RealPages real numbers and other metrics. Knowing that RealPage is not a public company, I was curious about the result. The tool came back with something that violates trust and ethics as a whole: a presentation with fake numbers and claims that are not backed up by any true evidence.

One of the most surprising findings during this evaluation was Canva Magic Studio’s tendency to generate entirely fabricated statistics and numerical claims in presentation content. Early AI systems were widely known for this type of hallucination, but many modern text-generation tools have significantly reduced the frequency of this problem. Because of that, it was unexpected to see such confident but unsupported numerical claims appear in Canva’s generated slides. This reinforced how important it is to verify AI-generated information, particularly when tools are designed for polished presentation outputs that may appear authoritative even when the underlying information is incorrect.

Broader Reflections

Personally, I set healthy boundaries with AI tools in both my academic and professional work. AI tools have a variety of benefits, as they have helped me understand concepts, catch grammar errors, and prepare for interviews. The boundary I have set, however, is that I will not let AI replace learning. That being said, AI is not a tool that should be used to generate papers or complete assignments. That would simply hurt me in understanding relevant class or work material, not help me. AI should be used as a tool to help us learn, not make us fall behind by doing the work for us. Reflecting on DCDA, AI tools can be used in both a positive and negative light. From a positive stance, AI is a tool that we can actively explore DCDA themes in because it is still developing. For example, being a senior in college, most of us currently in the program grew up in a world that revolves around technology: cell phones, wifi, big data, social media, and much, much more. Now, however, we are experiencing the development of a tool that is very new and currently on the rise: AI. DCDA explores topics like the Internet, ethics, cybersecurity, social media burnout, and all things digital. As AI develops, we have the privilege of being able to actively explore and evaluate these concepts within AI specifically. Unlike other tools, such as Safari, we were born into a world that was already reliant on it. With AI, however, we get to study these concepts and form our own opinion on the tool. I consider that quite a privilege. The negative side, however, is that a lot of people, unfortunately, do let AI steer them away from learning. It is becoming more and more talked about that students are relying on AI. And as for DCDA, AI raises a lot of concerns: ethics, reliance, and even obsession. As new AI tools continue to be released, this project has reminded me to ask questions before diving in: Are there limitations? Are there ethical safeguards? When should I be using this? Can I trust the tool? In my opinion, we should not be scared of AI. What we should be, however, is vigilant. We should ask the right questions, learn how and when to use it, and certainly not become reliant on it.
